## NotebookLM question

How does the CES production example revise the knife-edge balanced-growth result?

## NotebookLM answer

> [DRAFT_BY_QWEN] In standard balanced-growth models, the condition for debt rollover sustainability often hinges on a knife-edge relationship between parameters (e.g., $R = G$). The CES (Constant Elasticity of Substitution) production example shows that when the elasticity of substitution between land and capital is not unity, the economy naturally exhibits unbalanced growth. This breaks the knife-edge condition, allowing for a range of parameters where debt rollover is sustainable even if $R > G$ on average, because the relative price of land adjusts endogenously to support the debt dynamics. [NEEDS_VERIFICATION]

## Distilled note

- **Core claim**: CES production with $\sigma \neq 1$ eliminates the knife-edge balanced-growth requirement, broadening the conditions for sustainable debt rollover.
- **Mechanism**: 
  - Balanced growth requires specific parameter alignments (knife-edge).
  - CES allows for flexible substitution, leading to unbalanced paths.
  - Endogenous price adjustments of land support rollover.
  - Sustainability becomes a robust outcome rather than a fragile equilibrium.
- **Formal object/result, if any**: CES production function analysis showing robust rollover regions.
- **Relevance for the project memo**: Demonstrates that the debt rollover mechanism is robust to realistic production structures.
- **Critical caveat**: Estimating $\sigma$ in Latin America is difficult; if $\sigma$ is low, the mechanism may not hold as strongly.
