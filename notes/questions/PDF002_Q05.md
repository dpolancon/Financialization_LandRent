## NotebookLM question

How does the CES production example revise the knife-edge balanced-growth result?

## NotebookLM answer

> [NOTEBOOKLM] By introducing a Constant Elasticity of Substitution (CES) production function, Hirano and Toda (2025) revise McCallum’s (1987) "knife-edge" balanced-growth result by demonstrating that Pareto inefficiency and infinite debt rollover are indeed possible in an economy with land. McCallum’s conventional proof that land structurally forces the safe interest rate above the economic growth rate ($R > G$) implicitly relies on a Cobb-Douglas production function where the elasticity of substitution equals exactly one ($\sigma = 1$), which ensures that land rents and wages grow at the same balanced rate [1, 2]. The authors use the CES example to show that when this knife-edge restriction is relaxed—specifically, when labor productivity outpaces land productivity ($G > 1$) and the elasticity of substitution between the two strictly exceeds one ($\sigma > 1$)—the economy undergoes unbalanced growth [3, 4]. Consequently, the land asset's value becomes asymptotically negligible relative to aggregate endowments, allowing the natural interest rate to fall below the overall economic growth rate ($R < G$) and generating a Pareto inefficient equilibrium that permits infinite debt rollover [4].

## Distilled note

- **Core claim**: The CES production example proves that McCallum's "land efficiency" result hinges on a knife-edge mathematical restriction ($\sigma = 1$), and demonstrates that relaxing it to allow unbalanced growth ($\sigma > 1$) permits Pareto inefficient equilibria and sustainable infinite debt rollover.
- **Mechanism**:
  - McCallum's balanced-growth framework mathematically necessitates a production function with an elasticity of substitution exactly equal to one ($\sigma = 1$), forcing land prices and aggregate output to grow concurrently.
  - The authors' CES example introduces empirically plausible unbalanced growth by setting $\sigma > 1$ and a labor productivity growth rate strictly greater than land productivity ($G > 1$).
  - Under these broader conditions, fundamental land rents grow significantly slower than aggregate wages, causing the land asset to become asymptotically negligible relative to the overall economy.
  - This structural shift allows the natural interest rate to fall below the economic growth rate ($R < G$), generating a Pareto inefficient equilibrium that effectively behaves like a landless economy, enabling infinite debt rollover.
- **Formal object/result, if any**: Proposition 3.4 (proving a Pareto inefficient equilibrium arises under CES when $G>1$ and $\sigma>1$) and Proposition 4.1 (proving McCallum’s balanced-growth labor-share convergence necessitates a production function essentially restricted to Cobb-Douglas, corresponding to $\sigma=1$).
- **Relevance for the project memo**: critique, debt rollover
- **Critical caveat**: Applying this theoretical revision via CES production mechanics to Latin America requires external verification, as the stylized formalization abstracts entirely from the region's structural heterogeneity, informal labor markets, commodity-export dependence, and external debt vulnerabilities.
