## NotebookLM question

How do productivity changes generate instability, hysteresis, or stagnation traps?

## NotebookLM answer

> [NOTEBOOKLM] Productivity changes generate instability, hysteresis, and stagnation traps because they produce competing effects on capital accumulation: they increase wages (which increases savings) but also increase the return on capital (which decreases the savings rate if the income effect dominates) [1, 2]. When a positive, labor-augmenting productivity shock occurs, the steady-state capital level for the economic boom ($k^H$) increases [3]. However, the capital required for full employment ($k^f$), which dictates the upper boundary for multiple momentary equilibria ($\overline{k}$), can increase even faster [4, 5]. If this boundary overtakes $k^H$, an initially stable economic boom undergoes a state transition into a fragile one characterized by multiple momentary equilibria [4, 6]. In this fragile state, a sudden shift to bearish (pessimistic) expectations can cause a large-scale economic collapse [7]. If the collapse is deep enough, the economy falls into the basin of attraction of a stable, low-investment stagnation trap ($k^L$) [8]. The economy can remain permanently stuck in this trap with involuntary unemployment even if the original productivity shock was only temporary, thereby exhibiting strong hysteresis [8-10].

## Distilled note

- **Core claim**: Positive productivity shocks can paradoxically destabilize an economy by shifting it into a regime of multiple equilibria, where pessimistic expectations can trigger a collapse into a permanent stagnation trap, generating strong hysteresis even from temporary technological improvements.
- **Mechanism**:
  - A positive productivity shock raises wages (boosting total savings) but also raises the return on capital, which reduces the savings rate (due to a dominant income effect).
  - These changes unevenly shift the macroeconomic state boundaries; specifically, the upper boundary for multiple equilibria ($\overline{k}$) can increase faster than the high steady-state boom ($k^H$).
  - As a result, the multiple equilibria region overtakes the initially stable economic boom, transforming it into a "fragile boom" susceptible to expectation-driven collapses.
  - If expectations turn bearish, the economy crashes and can get pulled into the orbit of a stable, low-investment stagnation trap, remaining there permanently even if the initial productivity shock reverses (hysteresis).
- **Formal object/result, if any**: The condition for a state transition from state (a) to state (b) via labor-augmenting technological progress, where $\overline{k}$ exceeds $k^H$, formally evaluated at the threshold $(\frac{A}{\omega_2})^{\#\#} \equiv \frac{e/(1+n)}{[(1+\frac{a_1}{a_2}(1-\delta))/\frac{A}{\omega_1}]+(\frac{a_1}{a_2}-\frac{1}{1+n})}$.
- **Relevance for the project memo**: critique
- **Critical caveat**: The model explains instability and hysteresis strictly through closed-economy domestic life-cycle savings dynamics; applying these expectation-driven technological shock mechanisms to Latin America requires external verification regarding the region's exposure to exogenous global financial shocks, foreign debt cycles, and commodity terms-of-trade shifts.
