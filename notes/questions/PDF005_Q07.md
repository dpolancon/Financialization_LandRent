## NotebookLM question

What are the strongest limitations of the model’s institutional assumptions for peripheral economies?

## NotebookLM answer

> [NOTEBOOKLM] The strongest limitation of the model's institutional assumptions for peripheral economies is its reliance on a closed-economy framework where the agricultural sector naturally benefits from domestic industrial productivity gains through ever-rising agricultural prices [1, 2]. In the context of an open, globalized economy, the authors note that knowledge moves slowly across borders, forcing late-developing nations to specialize in agriculture according to their comparative advantage [2, 3]. This international division of labor deprives peripheral economies of the learning-by-doing and scale economies inherent to the manufacturing sector, structurally worsening their comparative disadvantage over time [2]. Furthermore, because "advanced" countries experience rapid productivity advances in their own industrial agricultural sectors, the global terms of trade ultimately shift against agriculture [2, 4]. Consequently, peripheral economies do not experience the rising agricultural prices predicted by the closed-economy model; instead, they face declining terms of trade that harm current incomes and severely limit their future potential for long-term growth [2, 4].

## Distilled note

- **Core claim**: The closed-economy assumption that agricultural prices will rise alongside industrialization fails for peripheral open economies, as free trade forces them into agricultural specialization while global terms of trade shift against them, permanently limiting their growth.
- **Mechanism**:
  - In an open global economy, peripheral countries that are slow to develop manufacturing are forced to specialize in agriculture based on comparative advantage.
  - This specialization deprives them of the endogenous growth externalities (learning-by-doing and scale economies) generated exclusively by the manufacturing sector.
  - Simultaneously, rapid technological advances in the agriculture sectors of advanced economies drive down global agricultural prices.
  - Consequently, peripheral economies suffer from declining terms of trade, breaking the closed-economy mechanism that would have transferred industrial wealth to the agrarian sector, thereby retarding their long-term growth potential.
- **Formal object/result, if any**: Not applicable
- **Relevance for the project memo**: critique
- **Critical caveat**: The authors explicitly address "developing countries" and the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis regarding declining terms of trade, but applying these exact model limitations to Latin America requires external verification regarding the specific integration of Latin American peripheral economies into contemporary global financial and trade systems.
