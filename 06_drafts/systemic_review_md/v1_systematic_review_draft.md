# Land, Credit, Overvaluation, and Debt Rollover: A Systematic Narrative Review of the Hirano-Stiglitz-Toda Branch

## Review question

How does the Hirano-Stiglitz-Toda literature explain the relationship between land-price inflation, credit expansion, unbalanced growth, productive capital formation, land overvaluation, and debt rollover, and what can be carried into a Fondecyt project on Latin American land-rent financialization?

## Executive synthesis

This review reconstructs a bounded branch of recent work associated with Hirano, Stiglitz, Toda, and coauthors. The branch is useful because it does not treat land as a neutral background asset. Land is a non-produced, durable, scarce asset that can operate at the same time as a factor of production, a store of value, and a collateral object. That combination is the source of the macro-financial problem. If land prices rise because land absorbs savings, credit, or speculative demand, measured wealth may increase without a corresponding increase in productive capacity. If credit is routed toward land or real estate rather than produced capital, the expansion of balance sheets can reduce long-run growth. If non-land productivity grows faster than land productivity, land prices can become systematically overvalued relative to land rents. If land changes the relation between returns and growth, it also changes the conditions under which debt can be rolled over.

The core argument is therefore not that "financialization increases land rent" in a generic sense. The more precise claim is that land valuation becomes macroeconomically central when credit, collateral, savings, and growth are organized around a non-produced asset. The literature gives four mechanisms: land speculation crowds out capital formation; real-estate credit and collateral amplify land-price inflation; unbalanced growth can make land prices separate from rent fundamentals; and land can modify R versus G and the possibility of infinite debt rollover. These mechanisms are theoretically powerful but institutionally thin. For the Fondecyt project, they should be reconstructed first on their own terms and only then translated into Latin American urban land-rent and financialization contexts.

## 1. Introduction: the object of the review

**P01.** The problem addressed by this literature is the macroeconomic meaning of land-price inflation. A rise in land prices is not equivalent to an expansion of productive capacity, because land is not produced in the way machines, infrastructure, or productive structures are produced. The distinction matters because the same price increase can appear as private wealth, collateral value, or capital gain while failing to add new productive capital. Stiglitz's wealth measurement papers provide the conceptual base: measured wealth can rise through capitalized rents, including land and monopoly rents, even when the underlying productive capital stock does not rise proportionally (Stiglitz, 2015c, pp. 1, 2, 4, 11; Stiglitz, 2015a, pp. 10, 11, 28).

**P02.** The review is organized around relations rather than around a paper-by-paper sequence. The relation between land and productive capital establishes the object. The relation between credit and land price establishes the financial channel. The relation between unbalanced growth and rent fundamentals establishes the overvaluation theorem. The relation between land, R, G, and debt rollover establishes the debt-sustainability channel. Wobbly dynamics is treated as a supplementary branch because it clarifies endogenous instability and phase transitions, but it does not replace the four central mechanisms (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022a, pp. 7, 24, 46, 47; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022b, pp. 1, 2, 10).

**P03.** The review follows a reconstruction-before-critique rule. The Hirano-Stiglitz-Toda branch is first read as a formal macro-financial research program. Its internal objects are land, land rent, land price, productive capital, wealth, collateral, leverage, R, G, and debt rollover. Its internal methods are overlapping-generations models, rational expectations, collateral constraints, no-arbitrage conditions, and unbalanced growth. The Fondecyt translation comes later: the models are not Latin American urban political economy, but they identify mechanisms that can be translated into that field if their institutional assumptions are made explicit.

## 2. Method and corpus

**P04.** The corpus consists of fourteen registered sources and two layers of NotebookLM-backed notes: ninety-eight per-paper question notes and thirty-two cluster synthesis notes. The notes were used as extraction aids, not as sources to cite. Claims enter this draft only when they are traceable to the registered source papers through the note system. Exact page references are preserved when available. When the notes support a claim but lack sufficient citation detail for final publication, the draft marks the problem explicitly rather than inventing a page or reference.

**P05.** The extraction protocol preserves five analytical dimensions. First, definitions identify the objects: land, land rent, housing rent, real estate value, productive capital, wealth, bubble, and overvaluation. Second, mechanisms explain how the objects are linked. Third, causality specifies the order of movement, such as credit easing to land demand to land prices to capital crowding-out. Fourth, variables and parameters identify what does the work in the model: P_t, D_t, K_t, k_{t+1}, R, G, interest rates, collateral parameters, leverage, and elasticity of substitution. Fifth, theory and evidence distinguish formal results, empirical motivations, and policy implications.

**P06.** The cluster synthesis is used as infrastructure rather than as final prose. Cluster C1 grounds the wealth-capital distinction. Cluster C2 organizes wobbly dynamics and instability. Cluster C3 organizes credit, low rates, and crowding-out. Cluster C4 organizes unbalanced growth, leverage, land bubbles, and overvaluation. Cluster C5 organizes land, R versus G, and debt rollover. Cluster C6 is treated as corpus-level synthesis, not as a normal bounded cluster; its claims are useful for cross-corpus interpretation but require special care because they draw on all sources at once.

## 3. Conceptual foundations: land, rent, wealth, and productive capital

**P07.** The first distinction is between productive capital and wealth. Productive capital refers to produced assets used in production. Wealth is broader: it measures ownership claims over resources and can include productive capital, land, capitalized rents, monopoly rents, intellectual-property rents, and other claims. This is why an increase in wealth is not automatically capital deepening. In Stiglitz's formulation, the wealth residual is precisely the gap between observed increases in wealth and the much smaller increase implied by real capital accumulation and national saving (Stiglitz, 2015a, pp. 10, 11, 28; Stiglitz, 2015c, pp. 1, 2, 4, 11).

**P08.** The second distinction is between land rent and land price. Land rent is a flow or dividend generated by land. Land price is the capitalized asset price of the land. The two can move together, but they need not do so. The Land Overvaluation Theorem is built around this separation: land is overvalued when its price exceeds the present value of its rents under the relevant discounting structure. This is not the same as saying that all high land prices are bubbles. It is a specific relation between a price and its rent fundamentals (Hirano and Toda, 2025b, pp. 1, 3, 4, 13).

**P09.** The third distinction is between land, housing, and real estate. Real estate combines land and produced structures. Housing rent includes the service flow from structures as well as the land component. Pure land rent is the return to the non-produced site. Toda's short paper is important because it shows why observed housing rent-yield ratios can be misleading. A stationary aggregate housing rent-to-price ratio does not rule out a land bubble if the analyst fails to separate the return on reproducible structures from the pure land dividend (Toda, 2025, pp. 1, 2, 3).

**P10.** These distinctions matter because they prevent a category error in the analysis of financialization. If a bank expands mortgage or real-estate credit and land prices rise, measured wealth and collateral values can rise immediately. But that does not mean the economy has produced more capital. Nor does it mean that the flow of land rent has grown at the same pace as the asset price. The SHT literature uses this distinction to show how a macroeconomy can look richer on balance sheets while becoming more exposed to lower productive investment, more fragile collateral values, or more unstable asset-price paths (Stiglitz, 2015b, pp. 16, 17, 18; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2024, pp. 1, 15, 33, 38).

## 4. Mechanism I: land-price inflation and capital-formation crowding-out

**P11.** The simplest crowding-out mechanism appears when land competes with productive capital for savings. In the OLG version, young agents allocate finite savings between capital and land. If the price of land rises, more of the savings envelope is absorbed by land purchases and less remains for real capital accumulation. Hirano and Stiglitz formulate this directly through the relation k_{t+1} + P_t = s_t(w_t + e): higher land prices reduce capital investment in the "obvious sense" that land holdings crowd out real capital accumulation (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022a, pp. 7, 24, 46, 47).

**P12.** The causal chain is not merely accounting. A land boom raises the asset value of a fixed asset. Agents treat land as a store of value, and expectations of resale sustain demand. Rising land prices reduce capital accumulation, lower future wages and output, and can raise the return on scarce productive capital. That higher return can then require still faster land-price appreciation for the boom to remain consistent with arbitrage. When the trajectory hits its bound, expectations can reverse and the boom can endogenously turn into a crash (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022a, pp. 7, 24, 46, 47).

**P13.** The Henry George paper gives the same mechanism a policy-theoretic framing. Land speculation diverts scarce savings away from productive investment; a tax on land or on the returns to land speculation can redirect resources toward productive capital and improve growth and welfare under the model's assumptions. The important point is not merely that land taxation raises revenue. It is that land taxation changes portfolio incentives in a setting where an asset of fixed supply competes with produced capital for savings (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2025a, pp. 327, 329, 335, 345).

**P14.** This crowding-out mechanism should not be flattened into the claim that every land purchase reduces growth. The model result is conditional. It depends on land functioning as an alternative store of value, on savings or financing being limited in the relevant margin, on land-price appreciation being attractive relative to productive investment, and on the purchase of land not creating new productive capacity. Those conditions are plausible in financialized land markets, but their empirical presence must be established in each context. For Latin American cases, this is a [source gap]: the current corpus does not itself provide evidence on the composition of credit, land tenure, informal housing production, or metropolitan land markets in the region.

## 5. Mechanism II: credit, collateral, leverage, and monetary policy

**P15.** The credit mechanism sharpens the crowding-out claim. Hirano and Stiglitz argue that the macroeconomic effect of credit expansion depends less on the aggregate amount of credit than on its sectoral allocation. Credit directed toward productive capital investment can raise productivity and growth. Credit directed toward real estate or land can fuel land speculation, inflate asset prices, and reduce long-run growth. This sectoral-credit hypothesis is stated explicitly in the long-run growth paper: it is "not so much aggregate credit expansion" that matters, but sectoral credit expansion (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2024, pp. 1, 15, 33, 38).

**P16.** The same mechanism appears in the low-interest-rate policy paper. Empirically, rapid credit expansion is associated with later growth slowdowns when credit flows disproportionately into real estate, while credit expansion toward manufacturing is associated with higher productivity. The model then explains why: lower rates or looser collateral constraints reduce the down payment required to acquire land, increase the leveraged return on land speculation, and shift portfolios away from productive capital. What looks like easier finance in partial equilibrium can become lower productive investment in general equilibrium once land-price effects are included (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2025c, p. 1; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2024, pp. 1, 15, 33, 38).

**P17.** The collateral distinction is central. Hirano and Stiglitz distinguish collateral constraints tied to productive capital from collateral constraints tied to land. Relaxing capital collateral can crowd in productive investment. Relaxing land or real-estate collateral can crowd out productive investment if it makes land speculation more profitable. The direction of the effect depends on the relative pledgeability of capital and land, often represented in the notes through theta for capital collateral and theta^x for land collateral (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2024, pp. 15, 33, 38; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2025c, [citation detail needed]).

**P18.** This is also where Stiglitz's land-and-credit analysis connects to the later Hirano-Stiglitz models. In Stiglitz's Part IV paper, credit availability and bank decisions can drive the price of land, raising measured wealth without increasing the equilibrium capital stock. Land operates as collateral, and the value of that collateral can itself be inflated by credit conditions. The mechanism is circular: credit supports land prices; higher land prices support collateral values; higher collateral values support more credit. The result is not productive accumulation but balance-sheet expansion around a non-produced asset (Stiglitz, 2015b, pp. 16, 17, 18).

**P19.** The financial channel does not imply that monetary policy is irrelevant. It implies that monetary policy is ambiguous once land is introduced. In a Tobin-style intuition, lower rates or higher liquidity push agents from money into productive capital. In the SHT branch, the asset menu includes land, and land can absorb liquidity that might otherwise fund capital formation. Expansionary policy can therefore be growth-enhancing or growth-retarding depending on whether it relaxes productive investment finance or land-collateral finance (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2025c, pp. 1-4; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2024, pp. 1, 15, 33, 38).

## 6. Mechanism III: unbalanced growth and the Land Overvaluation Theorem

**P20.** Unbalanced growth has a precise meaning in this branch. It refers to differential productivity growth between land and non-land factors or sectors. In the land overvaluation model, labor or human-capital productivity grows faster than land productivity. If the elasticity of substitution between land and labor exceeds one, the economy can continue expanding output while the productive contribution of land declines. Land rents grow slowly relative to wages and aggregate income, but land remains a durable store of value purchased out of rising savings (Hirano and Toda, 2025b, pp. 1, 3, 4, 13).

**P21.** The theorem's plain-language result is that land can become systematically overvalued even as its productive role declines. Formally, the notes record Theorem 1 as requiring sigma > 1 and a convergent expected infinite sum involving the ratio of labor productivity to land productivity. The economic interpretation is direct: because sigma > 1 makes the relevant exponent negative, faster growth in labor productivity relative to land productivity suppresses land-rent growth while rising income keeps pushing savings into land. Land prices are pulled up by savings faster than land rents can grow (Hirano and Toda, 2025b, pp. 4, 13).

**P22.** Land overvaluation is therefore not simply irrational exuberance. It is a model result in which the price of land separates from the present value of land rents under structural growth conditions. The theorem also differs from a generic housing-price boom. Housing combines land and structures; an observed housing rent-yield can remain stable even if the pure land component contains a bubble. Toda's measurement warning is therefore part of the same conceptual architecture: the analyst must separate the rent on structures from the pure land dividend before drawing conclusions about bubbles (Toda, 2025, pp. 1, 2, 3).

**P23.** The leverage and asset-bubble branch extends the overvaluation logic. Hirano, Jinnai, and Toda model a financial accelerator in which land prices, capital investment, land rents, wealth, and asset demand interact. Rising land prices can raise aggregate wealth and collateral values, which can feed investment and asset demand in a self-reinforcing process. This is not identical to the crowding-out mechanism in the Hirano-Stiglitz credit papers; the notes indicate a channel in which capital investment and land prices reinforce one another, generating a transition from fundamental equilibria to bubble equilibria under leverage and unbalanced growth (Hirano, Jinnai, and Toda, 2024, pp. 2, 3, 9).

**P24.** The resulting synthesis is heterogeneous. In one part of the literature, land speculation crowds out capital. In another, leverage and land-price increases can reinforce capital investment up to a phase transition. This is not a contradiction to smooth over. It means the effect of land-price inflation depends on the model architecture: whether land absorbs savings away from capital, whether collateral values relax investment constraints, whether leverage magnifies asset demand, and whether the equilibrium is fundamental or bubbly. A serious application to land-rent financialization must preserve these differences rather than citing "the literature" as if it made one uniform claim.

## 7. Mechanism IV: R versus G, land, and infinite debt rollover

**P25.** The R-versus-G branch begins from a conventional result. In overlapping-generations economies with land, McCallum and Homburg are read as showing that a productive, non-reproducible, non-depreciating asset such as land rules out the landless Diamond-type oversaving problem. Along a balanced growth path, land rents and prices grow with output; because land pays a positive rent, the total return on land exceeds the growth rate. By no arbitrage, the safe interest factor is pushed above the growth factor, R > G, and infinite debt rollover is ruled out (Hirano and Toda, 2025a, pp. 1, 2, 16, 17).

**P26.** Hirano and Toda challenge the generality of that result. They argue that the conventional land-efficiency conclusion depends on knife-edge balanced-growth restrictions, including equal productivity growth or Cobb-Douglas-like substitution conditions. Once the model allows unbalanced growth in which labor productivity grows faster than land productivity and sigma > 1, land can become asymptotically negligible relative to aggregate endowments. Under those broader conditions, a Pareto-inefficient equilibrium with R < G can arise even in an economy with land, reopening the possibility of infinite debt rollover (Hirano and Toda, 2025a, pp. 1, 2, 16, 17).

**P27.** The 2026 CIGS version adds a caution about the use of R < G as a policy shortcut. The notes indicate that the paper criticizes the heuristic that market frictions generating R < G automatically settle the debt-rollover question. If the definition of G, the asset return, and the relevant market frictions are not specified, the simple inequality can lose theoretical coherence (Hirano and Toda, 2026, p. 4). This point matters for the Fondecyt project because Latin American debt and land markets are open, institutionally heterogeneous, and exposed to currency, sovereign-risk, and external-finance conditions not represented in the closed OLG benchmark.

**P28.** The debt-rollover result should not be made to do more than it does. The formal result concerns infinite debt rollover in OLG economies with land and unbalanced growth. It does not by itself provide a theory of private developer balance sheets, mortgage refinancing, or corporate debt rollover in Latin American urban land markets. The executive memo's proposed translation, from OLG sellers to financiarized owners facing liabilities, maturities, liquidity pressure, and rollover needs, is therefore a project-level extension rather than a result already proven in the SHT corpus. That extension is promising, but it should be marked as translation rather than citation.

## 8. Wobbly dynamics and endogenous instability

**P29.** The wobbly-dynamics papers explain why land-price dynamics need not converge smoothly. In the global-dynamics branch, rational-expectations economies can fluctuate indefinitely without converging to a regular steady state. Adding land expands the parameter region in which these dynamics occur, because land is an alternative store of value that competes with capital and changes the relation among savings, capital accumulation, interest rates, and asset prices (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022b, pp. 1, 2, 10; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022a, pp. 7, 24, 46, 47).

**P30.** This branch contributes a theory of endogenous instability. The instability is not imposed from outside by irrational shocks. It follows from rational expectations, finite life, asset-market interactions, and phase transitions. A boom in land prices can be consistent with expectations for a time; as land absorbs savings, capital falls, wages and output fall, interest rates rise, and the required land-price appreciation becomes increasingly demanding. At a threshold, the boom is no longer sustainable and the economy shifts to a crash path (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2022a, pp. 7, 24, 46, 47).

**P31.** For the review's main argument, wobbly dynamics is supplementary. It gives a language for instability, bounded boom-bust paths, and endogenous crashes, but it does not replace the credit-allocation, overvaluation, or R-versus-G mechanisms. Its greatest value for the Fondecyt project is to prevent a static reading of land overvaluation. If land prices, credit, and capital formation interact dynamically, the relevant question is not only whether land is overvalued at one date. It is also how valuation paths affect investment, collateral, refinancing, and crisis timing. The specific translation to Latin American boom-bust paths remains a [source gap].

## 9. Policy implications inside the corpus

**P32.** The policy implication is not "less credit" in the abstract. The credit papers imply that composition matters. Credit to productive capital can crowd in investment and growth; credit to real estate or land can crowd out capital and retard growth. A policy agenda drawn from the corpus would therefore distinguish productive collateral rules from land-collateral rules, and would ask whether financial liberalization raises capital formation or mostly increases leveraged land demand (Hirano and Stiglitz, 2024, pp. 1, 15, 33, 38; Hirano and Stiglitz, 2025c, p. 1).

**P33.** Land taxation appears as a direct response to the speculative allocation problem. In the Henry George paper, land taxation is not only a distributional device; it alters the return to land speculation and can redirect savings toward productive capital. That policy claim remains model-dependent. It assumes that the relevant distortion is land speculation and that the state can tax land or land rents without reproducing other distortions. In Latin American settings, cadastres, informality, municipal fiscal capacity, political capture, and legal property regimes would have to be incorporated before the policy can be operationalized [citation detail needed].

**P34.** Monetary policy also has limits. Lower interest rates can support productive investment if the financial system channels liquidity toward capital formation. But lower rates can also raise the leveraged return to land and real estate. The SHT contribution is to show that low rates are not automatically pro-growth in economies where land is a major store of value and collateral object. The policy implication is targeted regulation: restrict the channels that transform liquidity into land-price inflation while preserving or expanding credit for productive investment.

**P35.** The policy section also requires a measurement warning. If wealth statistics rise because land values or capitalized rents rise, policy makers may mistake a balance-sheet expansion for improved sustainability. Stiglitz's wealth measurement work warns against that interpretation. A land-rent financialization agenda should therefore track not only asset prices and wealth-to-income ratios, but also productive capital formation, sectoral credit allocation, land-rent capitalization, and the separation between structures and land (Stiglitz, 2015c, pp. 1, 2, 4, 11; Toda, 2025, pp. 1, 2, 3).

## 10. Critical appraisal and Fondecyt translation

**P36.** The strongest contribution of the corpus is conceptual and mechanistic. It gives the project a disciplined way to say that land-price inflation can be macroeconomically harmful without relying on a moral claim about speculation. Land is fixed and non-produced; land prices can capitalize rents; land collateral can expand credit; credit can be routed toward or away from productive capital; unbalanced growth can separate land prices from rents; and debt-rollover conditions depend on the asset structure of the economy. These mechanisms are directly useful for the Fondecyt project's theoretical architecture.

**P37.** The weakest point is transportability. The models are stylized. They use OLG structures, rational expectations, closed or simplified economies, representative sectors, and abstract land markets. They do not directly model Latin American urban land tenure, informal settlement, developer finance, state planning, zoning, mortgage segmentation, dollarized property markets, extractive land rents, or peripheral financial dependence. These omissions do not invalidate the mechanisms. They specify the work required before those mechanisms can become claims about Latin America.

**P38.** The executive memo's most important translation concerns OLG. In the SHT branch, OLG solves the problem of trade in a valorizable asset: young agents buy, old agents sell, and resale expectations can support land demand. The Fondecyt project may not need to adopt OLG as its own ontology. It can translate the same problem into a political-economy account of owners, developers, households, or firms that sell land or real estate because they face liabilities, maturities, liquidity constraints, refinancing pressure, and balance-sheet management. That translation is not yet established by the corpus; it should be developed as a project contribution and marked as such.

**P39.** A second translation concerns unbalanced growth. In Hirano and Toda, unbalanced growth is a formal condition about differential productivity growth and substitution between land and non-land factors. In the project's own developing framework, unbalanced growth may refer to a non-proportional relation among demand, capital accumulation, and productive capacity. These are not the same concept. The review should use the SHT concept when reconstructing the theorem and reserve the project's broader concept for later theoretical development. Collapsing the two would create false continuity.

**P40.** The corpus also leaves empirical tasks open. It motivates questions about sectoral credit, real-estate collateral, land taxation, and land-price/rent separation, but it does not answer them for Chile or Latin America. A next empirical stage would need data on sectoral credit flows, land and structure decomposition, rent yields, collateral rules, mortgage and developer finance, property taxation, informality, and metropolitan price gradients. Until that work is done, the review can identify mechanisms and research hypotheses, not validated regional facts.

## 11. Conclusion

**P41.** The Hirano-Stiglitz-Toda branch is best read as a theory of how a non-produced asset can reorganize macro-financial relations. Land is not just another input. It is a store of value and collateral object whose price can rise without expanding productive capacity. That price increase can absorb savings, redirect credit, change collateral values, alter growth paths, and affect the relation between returns and growth. The branch therefore gives the Fondecyt project a rigorous way to connect land rent, credit, capital formation, overvaluation, and debt rollover.

**P42.** The most robust mechanisms are the wealth-capital distinction, the sectoral-credit distinction, the savings/capital crowding-out channel, the unbalanced-growth route to land overvaluation, the measurement distinction between housing rent and pure land rent, and the R-versus-G critique of conventional land-efficiency results. The more fragile elements are the direct application of OLG to Latin American owners, the leap from public infinite debt rollover to private balance-sheet refinancing, and any empirical claim about regional land-rent financialization not yet supported by regional data.

**P43.** The next phase should draft the final working memo by preserving this order: define the objects; reconstruct the mechanisms; state the causal chains; identify variables and parameters; distinguish theory from evidence; then translate. The review should not claim that SHT already explains Latin American land-rent financialization. It should claim that SHT provides a formal mechanism set that can be translated, tested, and modified for that project.
