# Cluster: Unbalanced growth, leverage, land bubbles, and overvaluation

## 1. Cluster scope

This cluster synthesizes the former cluster-question notes `C4_Q01.md` through `C4_Q05.md`. The notes cover PDF008, PDF001, and PDF012: leverage and endogenous unbalanced growth, the land-overvaluation theorem, and land bubbles despite non-vanishing rents.

The cluster explains the relation between unbalanced growth and bubbles/overvaluation, distinguishes the land-overvaluation theorem from leverage-driven bubble necessity, maps elasticity and leverage parameters, defines land overvaluation versus rational bubbles and observed housing-price inflation, and records the measurement warning about housing rents versus pure land rents.

## 2. Core claim

The central claim is that modern growth can separate land prices from land rents. In balanced-growth settings, land rents and land prices can move together. Under unbalanced growth, aggregate income, savings, wealth, or demand for land as a store of value can grow faster than the productive rent generated by land. This creates a rising price-rent ratio and makes land overvaluation or land bubbles structurally possible.

The notes identify two routes. One route is technological or sectoral: productivity grows faster in non-land or human-capital-intensive sectors than in land-intensive sectors, while land remains a store of value. The other route is financial: leverage relaxations can create a positive feedback between land prices, wealth, capital investment, rents, and further demand for land. PDF012 adds a crucial empirical warning: a stable housing rent-to-price ratio does not rule out a pure land bubble, because housing rents combine structure services with land rents.

## 3. Definitions

Land overvaluation is a systematic divergence of land price above rent fundamentals generated by structural model conditions. A rational bubble is an asset-price component not pinned down by the present value of dividends under rational-expectations dynamics. An asset bubble is broader language for price movements not justified by fundamentals. Observed housing-price inflation is an empirical movement in composite housing prices; it is not automatically the same as a pure land bubble.

Land rent is the flow return from land. Housing rent is the service flow from housing, which includes produced structures. Land price is the asset price of the underlying land. Fundamental value is the present value of land rents under the model's discounting structure. Leverage is borrowing capacity or balance-sheet amplification. Unbalanced growth is differential productivity growth across sectors or factors.

## 4. Mechanisms and relations

The structural route is: non-land productivity rises faster than land productivity -> aggregate income and savings rise faster than land rents -> land remains a fixed store of value -> demand for land raises land prices faster than rents -> price-rent ratios diverge -> land becomes overvalued relative to rent fundamentals. The source notes state the Land Overvaluation Theorem with conditions including `sigma > 1` and a summability condition involving `A_Ht/A_Xt` [page reference missing in source note for the exact theorem expression].

The leverage route is: relaxed leverage -> rising land prices and wealth -> greater capital investment and endogenous rent changes -> stronger asset demand -> further land-price increases. Once leverage crosses a threshold, the model can transition from balanced to unbalanced growth. This is not the same as the land-overvaluation theorem, but both routes explain how land prices can outrun rent fundamentals.

The measurement route from PDF012 is: housing rent combines structure rent and land rent -> aggregate housing rent-to-price ratios may appear stationary -> pure land rent-to-price ratios may still diverge -> empirical tests using housing rents can miss land bubbles.

## 5. Causality

In the land-overvaluation theorem route, differential productivity growth and sufficiently high substitutability between land and non-land factors are conditions under which overvaluation necessarily emerges in the model. In the leverage route, a leverage threshold is the condition for a phase transition into endogenous unbalanced growth. These are theorem-level or model-specific claims, not generic empirical laws.

The causal object is not simply "high prices." The claim is that the underlying cause is a divergence between the demand for land as an asset and the rent generated by land as a productive input. Feedback loops operate through wealth, investment, rents, and asset demand in PDF008, while PDF001 emphasizes structural transformation and the store-of-value role of land.

## 6. Variables and parameters

`P_t` denotes land price. `D_t`, `R`, or land rent denotes the flow return or dividend from land. The price-rent ratio is the key diagnostic object. `sigma` denotes the elasticity of substitution between land and non-land factors. `A_Ht` and `A_Xt` denote productivity terms for human/non-land and land-intensive production in the source-note theorem expression. `lambda` denotes leverage in the financial-accelerator route, with a critical threshold `\overline{\lambda}`. `G` denotes growth where used. Housing rents must be decomposed into structure and pure land components before being used as evidence.

## 7. Theory and evidence

This cluster is formal-theoretical with an empirical measurement warning. PDF001 is anchored in the notes at pages 1, 3, and 4 for the disconnection between declining land output and persistent land values. PDF008 is anchored at pages 2, 3, and 9 for the feedback between capital investment, land prices, rents, and asset demand. PDF012 is anchored at pages 1, 2, and 3 for the claim that stationary housing rent yields do not rule out land bubbles.

The source notes mention evidence on substitution elasticity and examples from US and European cities in the rent-yield discussion. Those should be treated as empirical motivation unless checked in the underlying paper.

## 8. Contribution to the systematic review

This cluster supplies the review's formal land-overvaluation core. It should be used to distinguish land overvaluation from housing-price inflation, and to explain why rent fundamentals and asset prices can diverge under modern growth. It also sets the empirical requirement: the review cannot infer absence of land bubbles from housing rent-yield stability unless it has separated pure land rents from structure rents.

## 9. Open issues and risks

The main risk is confusing overvaluation, rational bubbles, and observed housing inflation. The review should not call every price increase a bubble. A second risk is relying on housing rents as if they were pure land rents. A third risk is assuming that `sigma > 1`, leverage thresholds, or substitution through vertical construction apply in Latin American urban settings without checking zoning, informality, construction costs, and credit depth. The source notes explicitly flag the need for regional verification.
