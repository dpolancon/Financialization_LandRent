# Cluster: Land, R versus G, and infinite debt rollover

## 1. Cluster scope

This cluster synthesizes the former cluster-question notes `C5_Q01.md` through `C5_Q05.md`. The notes cover PDF002 and PDF014: two versions of the land, growth, return, and infinite debt-rollover argument. The cluster reconstructs the standard land-economy argument that `R > G` blocks infinite debt rollover, explains how unbalanced growth reopens rollover, clarifies how land bubbles can coexist with Pareto efficiency, critiques landless Diamond-style intuition, and records the version-control decision.

## 2. Core claim

The central claim is that the presence of land does not unconditionally close the door to infinite debt rollover. The conventional McCallum/Homburg-style argument says that productive, non-reproducible, non-depreciating land guarantees `R > G` on a balanced growth path. If the interest or return factor exceeds the growth factor, debt grows faster than the economy and cannot be rolled over forever.

The Hirano-Toda notes challenge that conclusion by showing that the standard result depends on restrictive balanced-growth conditions. Under unbalanced growth, especially when labor or non-land productivity grows faster than land productivity and substitution conditions hold, land bubbles can emerge. In that setting, infinite debt rollover can be possible even when the equilibrium is Pareto efficient. The 2026 CIGS version, represented by PDF014, should be treated as the main source for the final memo, while PDF002 remains useful for cross-checking the earlier formulation.

## 3. Definitions

`R` is the gross return or interest-return concept used in the debt-rollover argument. `G` is the growth factor or growth rate concept. Infinite debt rollover is the possibility of sustaining debt by continually issuing new debt rather than repaying principal from primary surpluses. Pareto efficiency is the dynamic-efficiency status of the equilibrium, not a claim that distribution is equitable.

Land rent is the dividend or flow return from land. Land price `P_t` is the asset price. Fundamental land value `V_t` is the present value of land rents under the model. A land bubble is the part of the land price not explained by fundamentals. Balanced growth means land rents, land prices, and output grow in aligned ways; unbalanced growth means those rates diverge.

## 4. Mechanisms and relations

The standard mechanism is: land is productive and pays a positive rent -> along balanced growth, rents and land prices grow with the economy -> total land returns include both rent and capital gain -> no-arbitrage aligns safe returns with land returns -> `R > G` -> infinite debt rollover is blocked. This is the baseline claim reconstructed in `C5_Q01.md`.

The revised mechanism is: balanced-growth restrictions are relaxed -> non-land productivity grows faster than land productivity and substitutability is high enough -> land prices can contain a bubble component -> the bubbly land price absorbs savings -> the government can convert part of what would otherwise be land-bubble value into public debt -> infinite rollover becomes possible even in a Pareto-efficient equilibrium [page reference missing in source note for this last conversion phrasing].

The cluster therefore links land valuation to sovereign financialization. Land is not just a productive factor in the argument; it is an asset whose bubble component can interact with public debt.

## 5. Causality

The conventional `R > G` result is conditional on balanced growth and related restrictions such as identical productivity growth rates or Cobb-Douglas-style substitution conditions. It should not be treated as an unconditional theorem about all economies with land. The source notes identify unbalanced growth and `sigma > 1` as conditions that reopen the possibility of bubbles and rollover [page reference missing in source note for the exact condition statement].

The important causal distinction is between dynamic efficiency and rollover feasibility. In Diamond-style landless intuition, infinite debt rollover is often associated with dynamic inefficiency. In these land models, the notes state that rollover can be possible even with Pareto efficiency because the bubbly asset component changes the asset-supply and savings-absorption logic.

## 6. Variables and parameters

`R` is the gross risk-free interest or return factor. `G` is the growth factor. `P_t` is land price. `r_t` or land rent is the land dividend. `V_t` is fundamental value. `sigma` is the elasticity of substitution between land and labor or non-land factors. Productivity growth by land and non-land sectors matters because unbalanced growth is the route through which the standard result fails. Public debt enters as the object whose rollover feasibility is being assessed.

## 7. Theory and evidence

This cluster is formal-theoretical. PDF002 is anchored in the source notes at pages 1, 2, 16, and 17 for the critique of the claim that land unconditionally guarantees Pareto efficiency and `R > G`. PDF014 is anchored at page 4 for the later CIGS version's critique of the simple `R < G` heuristic and its warning that realistic frictions can complicate the relationship.

The source notes identify PDF014, dated January 15, 2026, as the main source for final memo use because it is the newer and more refined version, while PDF002 remains a useful predecessor. That version decision is internal to the notes and should be verified against the bibliography before final publication.

## 8. Contribution to the systematic review

This cluster contributes a formal macro-finance result rather than a direct land-rent empirical mechanism. It should be used in the review to show that land affects the logic of debt sustainability, public finance, and asset bubbles. It also helps prevent an overly simple use of `R > G` or `R < G` heuristics in the presence of land.

## 9. Open issues and risks

The main risk is treating `R > G` or `R < G` as a portable empirical rule without reconstructing the asset structure. A second risk is collapsing Pareto efficiency into welfare desirability; the source notes' Pareto-efficiency claim does not resolve distributional or political-economy questions. A third risk is applying the result to Latin America without accounting for land inequality, informal property rights, sovereign risk, currency constraints, and financial segmentation.
