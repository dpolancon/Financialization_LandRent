Here is the concise synthesis based on the selected sources for your Fondecyt project on land-rent financialization:

### 1. The Cross-Paper Answer
Capitalized land rents generate increases in measured wealth without expanding productive capacity because standard macroeconomic metrics conflate **wealth ($W$)** with **productive capital ($K$)** [1, 2]. Wealth is a measure of the control over resources, heavily incorporating non-produced assets like land [2, 3]. Because the physical supply of land is fixed, an increase in its capitalized value—driven by falling interest rates, expanding credit, or its status as a positional good—inflates measured wealth independent of any actual accumulation of productive capital [4-6]. Consequently, wealth can rise significantly while the real productive potential of the economy stagnates or even declines [6, 7].

### 2. Key Mechanisms and Variables
*   **The Capitalization Formula ($W = K + R/r$):** Wealth consists of productive capital ($K$) plus the rent of land ($R$) divided by the interest rate ($r$). A permanent decrease in the real interest rate leads to an equiproportionate increase in land value, massively inflating wealth without adding a single unit of productive capital [8-10]. 
*   **Land as a Positional Good:** Because attractive land (e.g., beach-front property) is fixed in supply, it acts as a positional good. As the wealthy accumulate more income, they bid against each other for these fixed assets. The price of land ($p$) increases, elevating total wealth ($W = K + pT$) without yielding any additional output [11, 12].
*   **Credit Creation and Collateral:** The financial system preferentially uses land as collateral. An expansion of credit ($c$) targeted at landowners bids up the price of land, thereby increasing rentier wealth. This monetary expansion generates asset price inflation (a "wealth residual") that strictly enriches property owners but does not stimulate real capital investment [13-15].
*   **Bubble Paths and Crowding Out:** The dynamics of land valuation are prone to bubbles. On "bubble paths," the surging value of land can absorb savings, acting as a substitute for real capital accumulation. Wealth increases while the capital-labor ratio ($k$) actually decreases, essentially crowding out real productive capacity [16-19].

### 3. Tensions, Limits, and Open Questions
*   **Measurement Illusion vs. Welfare:** A major tension exists between national accounting and true economic sustainability. A surging wealth-to-income ratio driven by land bubbles masks underlying structural degradation, such as the destruction of human capital or "dark matter" unrecorded in standard metrics [7, 20-22].
*   **Endogenous Instability:** Without futures markets extending infinitely into the future, land markets are dynamically unstable. It remains an open question whether market economies can ever "correct" land bubbles without simply transitioning to a new bubble path [17, 23, 24].
*   **Latin American Applications [Requires Verification]:** The sources primarily model advanced economies (referencing the US, Europe, and the "Riviera") [6, 25]. The exact mechanisms of how land-rent financialization, credit availability, and the wealth-capital divergence operate within the specific institutional, banking, and inequality contexts of Latin America remain uncertain and require localized empirical verification. 

### 4. Relevance to the Final 5-15 Page Memo
This synthesis provides the fundamental macroeconomic and accounting framework for the Fondecyt memo. By firmly separating $W$ (wealth) from $K$ (productive capital), it validates the project's focus on land-rent financialization as a driver of inequality rather than growth [1, 2]. Furthermore, it offers a direct policy lever: it demonstrates theoretically that taxing the returns to land (or capital gains on land) lowers the equilibrium price of land and closes the gap between wealth and capital, potentially redirecting savings back into productive investment without harming economic output [12, 26, 27].
