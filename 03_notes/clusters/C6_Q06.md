### 1. The Cross-Paper Answer: The Memo Architecture
The final 5–15 page memo architecture for the Fondecyt project naturally structures itself around a paradigm shift: moving away from standard neoclassical models toward a framework where land (a non-produced asset) and credit frictions are the primary drivers of macroeconomic instability, wealth inequality, and stagnant productive growth. 

The architecture should follow a four-part structure:
*   **Part I: The Wealth Residual & The Land-Capital Distinction:** Establishing that the recent surge in wealth-to-income ratios is driven by capitalized rents (primarily land and real estate) rather than productive capital accumulation [1-3].
*   **Part II: The Inevitability of Land Bubbles:** Explaining how unbalanced growth (technological progress concentrated in non-land sectors) combined with high elasticity of substitution makes land overvaluation and endogenous boom-bust cycles ("wobbly dynamics") inevitable [4-6]. 
*   **Part III: Financialization and the Crowding-Out Effect:** Demonstrating how looser monetary policy and financial deregulation channel credit into land speculation rather than productive capital, stunting long-term growth [7-9].
*   **Part IV: Policy Solutions (The Henry George Revival):** Proposing heavy taxation on land rents and capital gains (building on Henry George's theories) and strict macroprudential financial regulations to redirect credit toward productive sectors [10, 11].

### 2. Key Mechanisms and Variables
To operationalize this architecture, the memo must detail the following core variables and mechanisms:
*   **Wealth ($W$) vs. Capital ($K$):** The sources emphasize that Wealth and Productive Capital are distinct aggregates. Wealth includes the capitalized value of exploitation rents, intellectual property, and, crucially, land as a positional good [1, 12, 13]. 
*   **Unbalanced Growth and Elasticity of Substitution ($\sigma$):** A key mechanism for land overvaluation requires an elasticity of substitution between land and non-land factors greater than 1 ($\sigma > 1$) [14]. When labor productivity grows faster than land productivity, land prices are pulled upward by growing incomes faster than land rents, causing explosive price-rent ratios and inevitable bubbles [15, 16].
*   **Credit Frictions and the Financial Accelerator ($\theta, \theta^x$):** The models differentiate between the collateralizability of capital ($\theta$) and land ($\theta^x$) [17]. Relaxing land collateral constraints ($\theta^x$) decreases the down-payment required for real estate, inducing a massive portfolio shift toward land speculation. This creates a **general equilibrium crowding-out effect** that diverts savings away from productive investments, slowing long-run economic growth [18, 19].
*   **"Wobbly" Macro-Dynamics:** Because the presence of multiple momentary equilibria depends on the endogenous state variable of capital stock, the economy undergoes "phase transitions." Land prices can endogenously crash when they exceed a critical threshold, leading to repeated, belief-driven boom-bust cycles without any exogenous shocks [5, 6, 20].
*   **Interest Rates ($r$) vs. Growth Rates ($g$):** Land economies resolve the standard dynamic inefficiency puzzle. In bubbly equilibria with land, infinite debt rollover (Ponzi schemes) becomes possible even when the safe interest rate is lower than the economic growth rate ($r < g$) [21-23].

### 3. Tensions, Limits, and Open Questions
*   **Tension with Standard Monetary Theory:** The traditional "Tobin effect" assumes that looser monetary policy (lowering interest rates) shifts portfolios from money to productive capital [8]. However, introducing land as a third asset subverts this: low interest rates fuel land speculation, exacerbating wealth inequality and choking productive growth [24, 25].
*   **Model Limitations:** The theoretical frameworks rely heavily on specific parameterizations (e.g., Overlapping Generations (OLG) models, logarithmic utility functions, and constant savings rates) [26, 27]. Moving away from these assumptions could complicate the comparative statics. Furthermore, distinguishing pure land rents from the rents of the housing structures built upon them remains an empirical challenge in national accounting [28]. 
*   **Open Questions for Latin American Application (*Requires Verification*):** The sources focus almost entirely on advanced economies (e.g., US, UK, Japan) [29]. Translating these models to a Latin American (Fondecyt) context requires verifying how these mechanisms function in environments with high labor informality, shallower credit markets, varying degrees of foreign land ownership, and distinct patterns of rapid, unregulated urban agglomeration [30]. 

### 4. Relevance to the Final 5-15 Page Memo
This synthesis directly dictates the narrative arc of the final memo. 

It provides the theoretical justification for why the Fondecyt project must treat land-rent financialization not merely as a symptom of inequality, but as the *root cause* of long-term macroeconomic stagnation. The memo will use the **"Wealth Residual"** to frame the problem [31], use **"Wobbly Dynamics"** and **"Unbalanced Growth"** to explain the underlying mechanics of Latin American real estate cycles [4, 5], and conclude with **Henry George-inspired policy recommendations**—specifically, implementing near 100% taxes on land rents and capital gains, combined with targeted credit regulations, to realign wealth accumulation with actual productive capital [10, 11, 32].
