# Cluster: Measurement, wealth-capital distinction, and rent capitalization

## 1. Cluster scope

This cluster synthesizes the former cluster-question notes `C1_Q01.md` through `C1_Q05.md`. The notes cover PDF009, PDF010, and PDF011: Stiglitz's measurement argument about wealth and sustainability, the Part IV land-and-credit argument, and the Part I wealth-residual argument. The cluster reconstructs the conceptual base of the review: measured wealth is not equivalent to productive capital, and land-rent capitalization can raise balance-sheet wealth without expanding productive capacity.

The synthesized questions are the wealth/productive-capital distinction, capitalization of land rents, the bridge from the wealth residual to later land-bubble and credit papers, measurement warnings for empirical work, and the proximity between Stiglitz's rent framework and heterodox language about rent extraction and financialization.

## 2. Core claim

The central claim is that the literature separates market-valued wealth from productive capital. Productive capital is the produced stock used in production, while wealth is a broader market valuation of ownership claims over resources. Wealth can include capitalized land rents, monopoly or exploitation rents, intellectual-property rents, and other claims that raise asset values without increasing productive capacity. This is the conceptual basis for the "wealth residual": observed wealth can rise faster than the accumulation of real capital implied by savings and investment data.

Land is the critical case because it is non-produced, fixed or scarce, durable, and often accepted as collateral. When the return to land is capitalized, lower interest rates, higher expected rents, or credit expansion can increase land prices and measured wealth even if no additional machines, structures, skills, or productive capacity are created. In the notes, PDF009 supplies the measurement warning, PDF010 supplies the land-credit channel, and PDF011 supplies the aggregate wealth-residual framing.

## 3. Definitions

Land is treated as a non-produced asset whose productive use, store-of-value role, and collateral role must be distinguished. Real estate is not identical to land; it combines land with produced structures and therefore can mix non-produced scarcity rents with produced capital. Land rent is the flow return or dividend generated by land. It must be kept separate from land price, which is the capitalized asset value of land, and from housing rent, which includes housing services and structure rents.

Productive capital is the produced stock used in production, commonly represented as `K`. Wealth, often represented as `W`, is a market-valued claim over resources. It includes productive capital but also capitalized rents and asset-price claims. Land-price inflation is an increase in land's asset price; it is not automatically evidence of capital formation. Land speculation refers to portfolio demand for land as an asset rather than as a productive input. Collateral value is the pledgeable value of land or real estate in credit creation. Leverage is borrowing or balance-sheet amplification linked to asset purchases.

## 4. Mechanisms and relations

The basic mechanism is capitalization. A stream of land rents is translated into an asset price, so measured wealth can be written in the notes' simplified form as productive capital plus capitalized rent, for example `W = K + R/r`. When `r` falls, the capitalized value of a given rent stream rises; when rents or expected capital gains rise, the land price rises; when credit is extended against land collateral, purchasing power is created for land acquisition. Each route can raise measured wealth without adding productive capital.

The land-credit mechanism deepens the measurement problem. Banks can expand credit against land collateral; higher purchasing power bids up land prices; higher land prices raise collateral values; the higher collateral value can support additional lending. This loop raises balance-sheet wealth and rentier claims while leaving the productive capital stock unchanged or lower if savings and credit are diverted away from productive investment.

The cluster therefore bridges measurement and macro-finance. It does not say that every increase in wealth is fictitious. It says that wealth aggregates must be decomposed before they can be used as evidence of capital deepening, sustainability, or growth capacity.

## 5. Causality

The direct causal claim is: capitalization of rents and collateralized credit can cause measured wealth to rise without causing productive capital to rise. Lower interest rates are sufficient in the simplified capitalization channel to raise the capitalized value of a given rent stream, but not sufficient to raise productive capacity. Land scarcity and land's role as a store of value are necessary background conditions for the land channel.

The feedback loop is: credit expansion against land -> higher land prices -> higher measured wealth and collateral values -> further credit capacity or speculative demand. The theorem-level status is limited in this cluster; the strongest formal expressions are accounting and model-specific decomposition claims, while later clusters supply theorem-level claims about overvaluation, bubbles, and debt rollover. Claims about Latin American applicability remain flagged in the source notes as requiring verification.

## 6. Variables and parameters

`W` denotes measured wealth or balance-sheet wealth. `K` denotes productive capital. `R` or land rent denotes the flow return to land. `r` denotes an interest or discount rate in the capitalization expression. `p` or `P_t` denotes land price. `T` denotes land in the Stiglitz-style notation where wealth may be written as `K + pT`. Credit `c` appears in the source notes as a claim against or financing relation tied to land ownership. Collateral value is not only an observed price; it is the part of land or real estate value that can be pledged in credit markets.

These variables should not be collapsed into one empirical proxy. In particular, land price is not land rent, real estate value is not pure land value, and aggregate wealth is not productive capital.

## 7. Theory and evidence

The cluster is primarily formal-conceptual and measurement-theoretical. PDF009 is used to motivate why wealth measurement matters for sustainability and inequality. PDF010 supplies a theoretical land-credit account in which credit availability affects land prices and measured wealth. PDF011 frames the wealth residual as the difference between observed wealth growth and measured capital accumulation.

The source notes contain page anchors for PDF009 pages 1, 2, 4, and 11; PDF010 pages 16, 17, and 18; and PDF011 pages 10, 11, and 28. Empirical facts in this cluster mainly motivate the theoretical problem; the notes do not present this cluster as an independent empirical test of land financialization.

## 8. Contribution to the systematic review

This cluster should anchor the definitions section of the systematic review. It supplies the review's warning against using wealth-to-income ratios, real estate values, or housing prices as direct evidence of productive capital accumulation. It also supplies the conceptual bridge to the later mechanisms: once land price is understood as capitalized rent and collateral value, credit expansion, leverage, land bubbles, and debt-rollover mechanisms can be read as variants of the same wealth-capital divergence.

## 9. Open issues and risks

The main risk is conflation. The review must not treat land, real estate, housing, land rent, housing rent, land price, and measured wealth as interchangeable. A second risk is empirical overreach: the notes motivate Latin American translation, but they repeatedly indicate that banking institutions, informality, tenure regimes, and regional land markets require independent verification. A third risk is page precision. Some formulas and cross-paper links are present in the former cluster notes without a single page anchor attached to each expression; those should be checked before final quotation.
