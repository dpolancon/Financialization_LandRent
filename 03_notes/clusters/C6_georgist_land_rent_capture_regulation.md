# C6 — Georgist land-rent capture and regulation

## 1. Cluster scope

This cluster synthesizes the literature-facing policy material in the former cluster-question notes `C6_Q01.md` through `C6_Q06.md`, with primary weight on the Henry George policy article and its links to the rest of the SHT/Hirano-Stiglitz-Toda corpus. It remains a literature cluster because its object is the corpus's account of land-rent capture, land taxation, capital-gains taxation, collateral regulation, and the redirection of savings away from speculative landholding.

The cluster does not treat "Henry George regulation" as a loose urban-policy label. It reconstructs Georgist land-rent capture and Georgist land-rent regulation as they appear in the notes: policy instruments that alter the return to landholding, reduce speculative demand for land, and change the allocation of savings, credit, and productive investment.

## 2. Core claim

The central claim is that land speculation is not only a distributional problem; in the SHT/Hirano-Stiglitz-Toda literature it is also a growth and welfare problem. When land absorbs savings and credit, productive capital formation can be crowded out. Georgist land-rent capture, capital-gains taxation on land, and financial regulation are therefore presented as ways to change macroeconomic allocation, not merely as fairness devices.

The Henry George policy article supplies the policy-facing synthesis: if land's private return attracts savings away from productive capital, then taxing land rents, taxing land capital gains, or publicly retaining land returns can reduce speculative incentives and redirect resources toward productive investment. The credit papers add a financial-regulation counterpart: if land is easier to pledge than productive capital, low rates and financial liberalization can intensify land speculation unless collateral and credit rules distinguish land/real-estate finance from productive finance.

## 3. Definitions

Georgist land-rent capture means public capture of the flow return generated by land when that return arises from scarcity, location, or social development rather than private production. Georgist land-rent regulation refers to the policy bundle that reduces private speculative returns to land through land-rent taxation, capital-gains taxation, public retention of land returns, and related regulation.

Land-rent taxation targets the flow return to land. Taxation of capital gains on land targets appreciation of the land asset. Public capture of land rents includes tax-based capture and, where explicitly present in the notes, public ownership or public retention of land returns. Financial regulation refers to collateral constraints, loan-to-value rules, and sectoral credit controls that reduce the preferential financing of land speculation. Productive capital is produced capital used in production; it must remain distinct from land price, land rent, collateral value, and measured wealth.

## 4. Mechanisms and relations

The policy mechanism is: land speculation absorbs savings and credit -> productive capital accumulation falls -> growth and wages suffer -> Georgist land-rent capture or land capital-gains taxation lowers the after-tax return to landholding -> savings are redirected toward productive capital -> long-run growth and welfare can improve. The source notes also state that a 100 percent land-rent tax combined with sufficiently high capital-gains taxation can approximate land nationalization with reinvestment in productive capital [page reference missing in source note for this exact formulation].

The credit-regulation mechanism is parallel. If land collateral is more pledgeable than productive capital, low interest rates or relaxed real-estate collateral rules raise the leveraged return to land speculation. Financial regulation can break that channel by restricting land collateralization relative to productive capital, tightening real-estate credit, or steering credit toward productive sectors.

The cluster therefore links Georgist land-rent capture to the earlier clusters: C1 explains why land-price gains inflate wealth without necessarily increasing productive capital; C3 explains why real-estate credit can crowd out productive credit; C4 explains why land prices can diverge from rents; and C2 explains how land speculation can amplify instability.

## 5. Causality

The causal claim is conditional. Georgist land-rent regulation improves capital formation only if the policy actually reduces the speculative return to land and if released savings or public revenues are redirected toward productive capital or public investment. Land-rent taxation, capital-gains taxation, and collateral regulation operate on different margins: flow rents, asset appreciation, and borrowing capacity.

The feedback loop the policy seeks to interrupt is: credit and savings enter land -> land prices rise -> measured wealth and collateral values rise -> further credit and savings enter land. The source notes also flag policy trade-offs: taxes may increase short-run volatility in some wobbly-dynamics settings, and land reform that gives workers freely tradable land can reduce welfare if it induces speculation rather than productive investment [page reference missing in source note for exact policy-tradeoff claims].

## 6. Variables and parameters

Key policy variables include the land-rent tax `tau^L`, capital-gains tax terms such as `hat{tau}` or `tau^{cg}`, collateral parameters `theta` and `theta^x`, the interest rate `r`, land price `P_t`, productive capital `K` or `K_t`, savings rate `s`, growth `g`, and the speculative share of resources such as `phi_t`.

For systematic-review use, these variables map to land values, pure land rents, land capital gains, real-estate credit, productive-sector credit, loan-to-value ratios, land tax rates, capital-gains tax rates, public investment, productivity, wages, and growth. They should not be collapsed into generic "urban policy" indicators.

## 7. Theory and evidence

This cluster is primarily formal-theoretical and policy-theoretical. It derives policy implications from model mechanisms rather than presenting a direct empirical evaluation of Georgist policy implementation. The strongest page anchors in the source notes include PDF005 pages 327, 329, 335, and 345 for the Henry George and land-taxation material; PDF003 page 1 and PDF013 pages 1, 15, 33, and 38 for sectoral credit and collateral channels; PDF001 pages 1, 3, and 4 for land overvaluation; PDF012 pages 1, 2, and 3 for rent-yield measurement; PDF009 pages 1, 2, 4, and 11; PDF010 pages 16, 17, and 18; and PDF011 pages 10, 11, and 28 for wealth measurement and land-credit links.

The policy claims should be cited as model-based claims unless the final review separately verifies empirical implementation evidence.

## 8. Contribution to the systematic review

This cluster contributes the policy and regulation section of the systematic review. It shows how the literature moves from diagnosis to instruments: tax land rents, tax land capital gains, capture land returns publicly, regulate real-estate collateral, and redirect credit toward productive capital. It also provides a way to evaluate policy proposals by asking whether they affect the relevant causal margin: speculative land returns, collateralized land credit, or productive capital formation.

## 9. Open issues and risks

The main risk is policy overreach. The notes identify land-rent taxation, capital-gains taxation, collateral regulation, financial regulation, and public investment, but they do not solve implementation questions about cadasters, state capacity, elite capture, informal tenure, zoning, or political resistance. A second risk is treating Georgist land-rent capture as a single instrument; the source notes distinguish flow-rent taxation, capital-gains taxation, collateral regulation, and possible public retention of land returns. A third risk is importing policy claims into the Fondecyt project without separating literature synthesis from project translation.
