# Cluster: Wobbly dynamics, phase transitions, and land-price instability

## 1. Cluster scope

This cluster synthesizes the former cluster-question notes `C2_Q01.md` through `C2_Q05.md`. The notes cover PDF006, PDF007, and PDF004: the baseline wobbly-economy model, the land-speculation extension with endogenous phase transitions, and a broader research-program account of growth and fluctuations with land speculation.

The cluster explains wobbly dynamics for non-specialists, shows how land changes the baseline OLG economy, defines phase transitions, reconstructs the dynamic crowding-out mechanism, and separates what is essential for the systematic review from background nonlinear-dynamics detail.

## 2. Core claim

The central claim is that an economy can generate persistent, rational-expectations boom-bust movement without relying on exogenous shocks, irrationality, or regular cycles. In the notes, "wobbly dynamics" names a dynamic path in which the economy does not converge smoothly to a steady state and does not follow a fixed periodic cycle. Expectations can be self-fulfilling because savings, interest rates, land prices, and productive capital formation interact nonlinearly.

Land makes the mechanism more relevant to land-rent financialization because land is an alternative store of value. Savings that could finance productive capital can instead be absorbed by land purchases. Rising land prices reduce capital accumulation, reshape future returns, and can push the economy toward thresholds where one type of equilibrium disappears and another becomes the only rational continuation.

## 3. Definitions

Wobbly dynamics refers to non-convergent and non-periodic rational-expectations dynamics in an OLG setting. A momentary equilibrium is an equilibrium at a date conditional on expectations about future prices and returns. Multiple momentary equilibria are states in which more than one expectation-consistent outcome is possible. A phase transition is a switch between dynamic regimes when the economy crosses a critical boundary.

Land is a non-produced store of value competing with productive capital for finite savings. Land price `P_t` is the asset price of land. Productive capital `k_{t+1}` is next-period capital formed from current savings. Land speculation is the use of savings to hold land for expected returns rather than to increase productive capacity. A boom is an expectation-consistent rise in land prices; a crash is an endogenous switch when the boom path hits a model-implied bound.

## 4. Mechanisms and relations

The basic crowding-out mechanism is the savings allocation constraint identified in the source notes: `k_{t+1} + P_t = s_t(w_t + e)` [page reference missing in source note for this exact equation]. With savings fixed at a point in time, a higher land price absorbs resources that otherwise would finance productive capital. Lower productive capital then changes wages and interest rates, which feed back into savings behavior and expectations.

The wobbly mechanism links expectations to realized returns. If agents expect a low interest rate, they may save more to reach retirement targets; higher savings can lower the realized return, validating the expectation. If expectations shift, a different equilibrium can occur. Land adds an asset-price channel: bullish expectations can raise land demand and price, but higher `P_t` crowds out `k_{t+1}`. As productive capital falls, the path becomes bounded by critical thresholds. When land prices reach an upper boundary, the boom path can no longer be sustained and a bearish/crash regime becomes rational. When the crash reaches a lower boundary, the economy can move back toward a bullish state.

The later credit-friction logic is adjacent but not the core of this cluster. The source notes mention that low-interest-rate and collateral channels are needed to map the theory onto real-world booms associated with low rates, but those mechanisms are treated more directly in Cluster C3.

## 5. Causality

Necessary conditions in the source notes include finite-lived OLG agents, savings behavior that can respond inversely to expected interest rates, and expectation-dependent momentary equilibria. Land is not necessary for the baseline wobbly economy, but it changes the dynamics by creating a non-produced asset that absorbs savings. Land-price increases cause lower next-period productive capital within the savings-allocation mechanism. Lower productive capital changes returns and wages, which alters future savings and price paths.

The phase-transition claim is model-specific and formal: when critical thresholds are crossed, the prior dynamic regime ceases to be feasible and the economy switches regime. The broader claim that land speculation can create boom-bust instability is a theoretical synthesis, not an empirical test in these notes.

## 6. Variables and parameters

`s_t` is the savings rate. `r_{t+1}` is the next-period interest rate or return. `P_t` is land price. `k_{t+1}` is next-period productive capital. `w_t` is wages or labor income in the savings expression. `e` is an endowment term in the source-note equation. Critical thresholds include an upper land-price boundary, written in the notes as `\hat{P}`, and lower boundaries such as `P_min` or `\underline{P}`. The source notes also use a speculative ratio `\phi_t = P_t/(AK_t)` and a growth expression `1+g_t = [s(1-\alpha)-\phi_t]A` [page reference missing in source note for these exact formulas].

## 7. Theory and evidence

This cluster is theoretical. PDF006 provides the baseline wobbly-economy argument. PDF007 provides the land-speculation and endogenous phase-transition extension. PDF004 situates the mechanism in a broader program on growth and fluctuations with land speculation. The notes contain page anchors for PDF004 pages 3, 4, 9, 17, 34, and 49; PDF006 pages 1, 2, and 10; and PDF007 pages 7, 24, 46, and 47.

## 8. Contribution to the systematic review

This cluster contributes the instability architecture of the review. It allows the review to argue that land speculation is not only a static diversion of savings but also a dynamic source of endogenous instability. It should support sections on mechanisms, causal architecture, and formal results. The technical nonlinear details should be used selectively; the review needs the phase-transition intuition, not every formal path condition.

## 9. Open issues and risks

The main risk is overstating empirical applicability. The source notes flag that the baseline model can associate booms with high interest rates, while real-world real-estate booms often involve low nominal interest rates. Credit frictions and collateral constraints are required to close that gap. A second risk is translating rational-expectations phase transitions into empirical language without evidence capable of distinguishing them from behavioral or institutional bubble explanations. Latin American applications remain uncertain because the models abstract from informality, spatial heterogeneity, and institutional credit differences.
