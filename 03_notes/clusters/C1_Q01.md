Here is a synthesis of the distinction between wealth and productive capital based on the provided Stiglitz (2015) papers, tailored for your Fondecyt project on land-rent financialization. 

### 1. The Cross-Paper Answer: Defining Wealth vs. Productive Capital
Across the sources, the cleanest definition of the difference is that **productive capital ($K$)** refers to actual produced physical assets (like "machines") that serve as relevant inputs into the production process to generate real economic output [1-3]. 

In contrast, **wealth ($W$)** is a broader financial metric representing a capitalized measure of ownership and "control over resources" [4]. Wealth includes not only productive capital but also the capitalized value of non-produced assets and economic rents—such as land rents, intellectual property, and "exploitation" or monopoly rents [1, 2, 5]. Because they are distinctly different aggregates, the value of wealth can soar even while the true productive capacity of the economy (capital) stagnates or declines [6-8]. 

### 2. Key Mechanisms and Variables
The divergence between wealth and capital is driven by several mechanisms:
*   **Capitalization of Land Rents:** Output can be modeled as $Q = F(K,L) + R$, where $R$ is the return to land. Wealth is then $W = K + R/r$ (where $r$ is the interest rate) [9, 10]. A decrease in the interest rate or an increase in the scarcity value of land increases $W$ exponentially, without adding a single unit to the productive physical capital $K$ [9, 11]. 
*   **Credit and Collateral (Financialization):** Land serves as a prime positional good and collateral for bank credit. As banks expand credit based on land collateral, land prices ($p$) are bid up. This increases the wealth of land-owning rentiers ($W^T = pT - c$, where $c$ is credit) without increasing the equilibrium capital stock, meaning wealth accumulation simply crowds out real capital accumulation [12-14].
*   **Exploitation and Monopoly Rents:** Increases in corporate market power, predatory lending, or government bailouts for "too-big-to-fail" banks are capitalized into financial wealth. This inflates $W$ but actually reflects distortions that *diminish* the true productive potential of the economy [15-18]. 

### 3. Tensions, Limits, and Open Questions
*   **Index Number and Aggregation Problems:** Measuring aggregate input ($C$) by mathematically combining capital ($K$) and land ($T$) is contentious. Depending on the elasticity of substitution, the aggregate productive input could be falling rapidly even as standard measures of wealth rise [19-21].
*   **Unmeasured "Dark Matter" (Missing Capital):** Existing wealth metrics fail to capture the destruction of human, organizational, and social capital during economic downturns (e.g., lost skills from youth unemployment or early retirement). Consequently, standard wealth frameworks severely understate the loss of future productive capacity [22-25].
*   **Geographic Applicability (*Requires verification for Latin American applications*):** Stiglitz explicitly models these dynamics around advanced economies (e.g., US/Europe data, Riviera land examples) [23, 26, 27]. How these rent-capitalization and credit-collateral dynamics map onto Latin American land-rent financialization—which features different banking regulations, informal markets, and natural resource dependencies—remains an open question requiring regional empirical verification [28]. 

### 4. Relevance to the Final 5-15 Page Memo
This distinction is foundational for your memo's theoretical framework. It provides the analytical proof that a rising wealth-to-income ratio does not equate to economic progress or "capital deepening" [6, 28, 29]. Instead, it demonstrates that land-rent financialization is largely an extractive process: the financial system generates credit that inflates land prices, redistributing wealth toward property owners and capitalists without increasing the real wages or productive capacity of the broader economy [14, 30, 31]. 

Furthermore, this W vs. K distinction directly justifies specific policy interventions for your project, such as demonstrating mathematically how taxing land rents or capital gains can reduce speculative wealth inequality and redirect savings back toward productive capital ($K$) [32, 33]
