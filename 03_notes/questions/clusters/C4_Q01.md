### 1. The Cross-Paper Answer
Across the selected literature, **unbalanced growth is the fundamental, structural engine that necessitates the emergence of land bubbles and overvaluation**. In a stationary economy characterized by balanced growth, land prices and rents grow at the same rate, tying asset prices to their fundamental values [1-3]. However, when an economy shifts to a nonstationary state of unbalanced growth, aggregate economic wealth—and therefore the demand for land as a store of value—grows faster than the land rents generated from its use as a production factor [2, 4]. This divergence structurally forces the price-to-rent ratio to rise without bound, making land overvaluation inevitable [2, 4]. This unbalanced growth is driven either by the relaxation of financial leverage constraints [2, 5] or by uneven technological progress across economic sectors [6, 7].

### 2. Key Mechanisms and Variables
The formal link between unbalanced growth and land bubbles relies on several critical variables and mechanisms:
*   **The Financial Accelerator and Leverage ($\lambda$):** In a macro-finance model, whether an economy experiences balanced or unbalanced growth depends on financial leverage [8, 9]. When leverage crosses a critical threshold ($\overline{\lambda}$), the positive feedback loop between capital investment and land prices becomes explosive [2]. The economy undergoes a "phase transition" to endogenous unbalanced growth, where purely demand-driven land prices outpace fundamental supply-driven land rents [2, 5, 10, 11]. 
*   **The Elasticity of Substitution ($\sigma$):** A necessary condition for these bubbles is that the elasticity of substitution between land and non-land factors strictly exceeds 1 at high input levels [12-15]. This ensures that as capital or labor accumulates, the marginal product of land (rent) does not grow as fast as aggregate output, suppressing rent growth relative to asset demand [4, 12, 16]. 
*   **The Land Overvaluation Theorem:** Formally, if human/intangible capital productivity ($A_{Ht}$) grows faster than land productivity ($A_{Xt}$)—satisfying $E_0\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}(A_{Ht}/A_{Xt})^{1/\sigma-1} < \infty$ with $\sigma > 1$—land will mathematically always be overvalued [4, 14, 17]. This applies to structural transformations, such as moving from a Malthusian agricultural economy to a modern knowledge-based economy [6, 18, 19], or during urban formation [20, 21].
*   **Separation of Land vs. Structure Rents:** Even if empirical researchers observe a stationary *housing* rent-to-price ratio (often used to dismiss bubble theories), a pure land bubble still inevitably emerges alongside economic growth. Because the rent on housing structures can mask pure land rent dynamics, failing to formally distinguish the two hides the underlying land overvaluation [22-24]. 

### 3. Tensions, Limits, and Open Questions
*   **Theoretical Sensitivity:** The inevitability of bubbles in these frameworks strongly depends on the assumption that the elasticity of substitution ($\sigma$) remains greater than 1 [12, 14, 25]. If $\sigma < 1$, interest rates would exhibit pathological, explosive behavior, breaking the models' core predictions [16, 17, 26].
*   **Empirical Measurement:** Validating these models requires precisely separating pure land rents from structure rents, which remains a significant empirical and measurement challenge [23, 24].
*   **Latin American Context:** **[Requires verification for Latin American applications]** The models assume frictionless structural transitions to high-productivity, knowledge-based sectors [19, 27], and rely heavily on endogenous growth fueled by leveraged capital investments [5, 9, 28]. Whether these precise formal threshold mechanisms ($\overline{\lambda}$) hold in Latin American economies—which often face shallow domestic credit markets, different sectoral compositions (e.g., commodity dependence), and constrained technological transition—remains an open question requiring regional empirical verification.

### 4. Relevance to the Final 5-15 Page Memo
For the Fondecyt project on land-rent financialization, these papers provide the definitive theoretical core for the memo: **land bubbles are not merely the result of irrational psychological manias or short-term frictions, but are a structural necessity of modern capitalist growth** [1, 3, 7]. 
This framework allows the memo to pivot from behavioral explanations of real estate speculation to structural macro-financial drivers. By anchoring the analysis in the "Land Overvaluation Theorem" [6] and the leverage-induced "phase transition" [2, 11], the memo can convincingly argue that financial deepening (loosening leverage) and technological modernization inherently generate a gap between land's productive value (rent) and its financialized value (asset price), perfectly bridging the concepts of unbalanced growth and land-rent financialization.
