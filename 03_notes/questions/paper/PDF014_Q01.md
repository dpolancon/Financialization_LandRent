## NotebookLM answer

> [NOTEBOOKLM] The CIGS version (PDF014, 2026) updates the earlier preprint (PDF002, 2025) by explicitly attacking the theoretical coherence of the "$G$ versus $R$" heuristic for public debt sustainability, reflecting its updated title "Land, G versus R, and Infinite Debt Rollover". While the earlier version established that basic land models mathematically force the interest rate above the growth rate ($R > G$), precluding Ponzi schemes, the CIGS version adds a crucial theoretical clarification regarding frictions [1, 2]. It acknowledges that introducing "realistic factors" like market segmentation or specific frictions into land models might mathematically allow the interest rate to fall below the growth rate ($R < G$) even under balanced growth [2]. However, the authors argue that in such friction-heavy environments, the feasibility of infinite debt rollover depends entirely on the specific nature of the frictions involved and can no longer be reduced to the simplistic $G$ versus $R$ relationship [2]. Consequently, they conclude that linking public debt rollover exclusively to $G$ versus $R$ derived from landless models fails to provide a coherent theoretical framework for macroeconomic policy [2].

## Distilled note

- **Core claim**: The CIGS version adds an explicit critique of the $R < G$ heuristic, arguing that even if market frictions allow $R < G$ in a land model, the simple $G$ versus $R$ condition loses its theoretical coherence and is no longer a sufficient metric for determining the sustainability of infinite debt rollover.
- **Mechanism**:
  - Standard landless models reduce the condition for infinite debt rollover to a simple metric: whether the safe interest rate is below the economic growth rate ($R < G$).
  - Basic land models mathematically force $R > G$, precluding infinite debt rollover.
  - Adding realistic frictions or market segmentation to land models might artificially depress the interest rate such that $R < G$ under balanced growth.
  - However, once these specific frictions are introduced, the conditions for sustainable debt rollover are dictated by the frictions themselves, rendering the simplistic "$G$ versus $R$" comparison theoretically incoherent as a standalone policy metric.
- **Formal object/result, if any**: Not applicable
- **Relevance for the project memo**: debt rollover, critique
- **Critical caveat**: Because this critique of the $G$ versus $R$ framework is highly theoretical and generalized, applying it to Latin America requires external verification to determine how the region's specific peripheral frictions—such as sovereign risk premiums, dollarized real estate, and informal property markets—interact with debt rollover sustainability.
