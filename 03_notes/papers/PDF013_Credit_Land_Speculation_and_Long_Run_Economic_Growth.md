## 3. Core thesis

- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q01` (1, 15, 33, 38): The sectoral-credit hypothesis asserts that credit expansions enhance long-run economic growth only when directed to productive sectors (like manufacturing), whereas credit expansions directed toward real estate fuel land speculation and structurally retard long-run growth.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q02` (2, 4, 5, 6): The model separates the two sectors by designating manufacturing as the sole engine of endogenous growth through capital accumulation, while defining real estate as a passive sector that utilizes fixed land and relies entirely on productivity spillovers from manufacturing.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q03` (4, 7, 10, 15): Credit constraints limit borrowing based on specific collateral parameters for capital ($\theta$) and land ($\theta^x$), which dictate investment allocation by determining the leveraged return and required down-payment for each asset.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q04` (2, 3, 15, 16): Lowering collateral requirements or interest rates can reduce growth because the resulting credit expansion fuels land speculation, which diverts finite savings away from productive capital investment.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q05` (3, 29, 30, 50): The finite-land-price puzzle is resolved by assuming limited real estate market participation, which forces land to be discounted at the entrepreneurs' high leveraged rate of return on capital rather than the low safe interest rate.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q06` (4, 19, 21, 27): The small-open economy relies on a fixed exogenous interest rate, whereas the closed economy endogenizes the interest rate through fiat money, which fundamentally amplifies the crowding-out effect of land speculation and enables the analysis of monetary policy.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q07` (1, 15, 31, 33): Private credit expansions do not inherently boost economic growth; instead, when channeled through lower interest rates or relaxed collateral constraints, they frequently trigger land-price inflation that crowds out productive capital investment and retards long-run growth.

## 6. Causal chains

- [SOURCE RESULT] `CLM_PDF013_Q04_001` (2, 3, 15, 16; validated_paraphrase): Lowering collateral requirements or interest rates can reduce growth because the resulting credit expansion fuels land speculation, which diverts finite savings away from productive capital investment.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `CLM_PDF013_Q04_002` (2, 3, 15, 16; validated_paraphrase): Proposition 3, demonstrating mathematically that $sign\frac{d(1+g^)}{dr} = sign(\theta^x - \theta)$, meaning lower interest rates actually reduce growth when $\theta^x \theta$ because the crowding-out effect dominates.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `CLM_PDF013_Q04_005` (2, 3, 15, 16; validated_paraphrase): Because land serves as excellent collateral (especially if its collateral parameter $\theta^x$ is greater than capital's $\theta$), entrepreneurs channel this new leverage into buying fixed real estate rather than producing capital.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `CLM_PDF013_Q05_006` (3, 29, 30, 50; validated_paraphrase): Because entrepreneurs arbitrage their borrowed funds between land and capital, land must offer a leveraged rate of return equal to the high returns of productive capital.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `CLM_PDF013_Q05_007` (3, 29, 30, 50; validated_paraphrase): This high leveraged return replaces the safe interest rate as the effective discount rate for land; because it strictly exceeds the economic growth rate, the present value of land remains perfectly finite even when the safe rate is extremely low.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `CLM_PDF013_Q06_002` (4, 19, 21, 27; validated_paraphrase): The endogenous interest rate condition $1+r^ = \frac{1+g^}{1+\mu}$, which links the interest rate to the money growth rate $\mu$, and the theoretical result that $\frac{d(1+g^)}{d\theta^x} < 0$ is amplified because the derivative includes the endogenous fall in $r$.

## 8. Formal results / model results

- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q01` (1, 15, 33, 38): Propositions 2 and 6, demonstrating mathematically that $\frac{d(1+g^*)}{d\theta^x} < 0$ (relaxing land collateral requirements $\theta^x$ retards long-run growth $g^*$) while $\frac{d(1+g^*)}{d\theta} > 0$ (relaxing capital collateral requirements $\theta$ enhances long-run growth).
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q02` (2, 4, 5, 6): The aggregate production function $Y_t = (K_t)^\alpha(\chi(K_t)L_t)^{1-\alpha} + D_t X_t$, combined with the real estate spillover function $D_t = \epsilon \chi(K_t) = \epsilon a K_t$, demonstrating that land dividends ($D_t$) rely on manufacturing capital ($K_t$).
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q03` (4, 7, 10, 15): The borrowing constraint equation $(1+r)b_t^i \le \theta R_t^c k_{t+1}^i + \theta^x R_t^x P_t x_t^i$, and the leveraged return arbitrage equation $\frac{R^c(1-\theta)}{1-\frac{\theta R^c}{1+r}} = \frac{R_t^x(1-\theta^x)}{1-\frac{\theta^x R_t^x}{1+r}}$.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q04` (2, 3, 15, 16): Proposition 3, demonstrating mathematically that $sign\frac{d(1+g^*)}{dr} = sign(\theta^x - \theta)$, meaning lower interest rates actually *reduce* growth when $\theta^x > \theta$ because the crowding-out effect dominates.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q05` (3, 29, 30, 50): Proposition 10, demonstrating that $R^c > 1+g^* > 1+r^*$, and the effective land discount rate $\frac{\lambda}{1-\theta^x+\frac{\theta^x\lambda}{1+r^*}}$, which replaces the standard $1+r^*$.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q06` (4, 19, 21, 27): The endogenous interest rate condition $1+r^* = \frac{1+g^*}{1+\mu}$, which links the interest rate to the money growth rate $\mu$, and the theoretical result that $\frac{d(1+g^*)}{d\theta^x} < 0$ is amplified because the derivative includes the endogenous fall in $r$.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF013_Q07` (1, 15, 31, 33): The relative size of land speculation $\phi_t \equiv \frac{P_t}{AK_t}$, and the comparative dynamic $\frac{d(1+g^*)}{d\theta^x} < 0$, which proves mathematically that relaxing land collateral limits ($\theta^x$) increases land speculation and unambiguous retards the long-run growth rate ($g^*$).
