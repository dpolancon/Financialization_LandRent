## 3. Core thesis

- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF005_Q01` (327, 329, 335, 345): The Henry George question is reconstructed to analyze how land speculation dynamically crowds out productive capital in endogenous growth models, proving that Georgist land taxation and strict financial regulations are necessary to prevent credit-fueled real estate booms from permanently stunting long-term macroeconomic growth.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF005_Q02` (326-328, 331, 333-335): The paper isolates the macroeconomic harm of land speculation by contrasting a rentier economy against a land-reform economy, ultimately proving that policy counterfactuals like Georgist taxation or full land nationalization are required to prevent speculative real estate from stunting economic growth.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF005_Q03` (327, 332, 333, 337): Land speculation reduces long-run welfare by absorbing finite domestic savings, creating a crowding-out effect that starves the real economy of productive capital, slows endogenous growth, and depresses future wages.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF005_Q04` (327, 328, 335, 344): To prevent land speculation from crowding out productive capital, governments must deploy fiscal policies (land-rent and capital-gains taxes), structural policies (land nationalization), or macroprudential financial regulations (differentiated collateral constraints).
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF005_Q05` (327, 343, 344): Challenging the Kiyotaki-Moore framework, the paper demonstrates that increasing the collateral value of land retards economic growth, because the resulting surge in leveraged land speculation crowds out productive capital investment in general equilibrium.
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q06` (327, 353, 356): The paper's dual-economy model validates Latin American structuralist concerns (explicitly citing Prebisch) by demonstrating that without strict Georgist taxation, land reform and financial liberalization will fuel unproductive land speculation, suppress industrial capital accumulation, and exacerbate structural disadvantages in agrarian economies.
- [SOURCE RESULT] `PDF005_Q07` (353, 354): The closed-economy assumption that agricultural prices will rise alongside industrialization fails for peripheral open economies, as free trade forces them into agricultural specialization while global terms of trade shift against them, permanently limiting their growth.

## 11. Limits and caveats

- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q01`: Evaluating this Georgist framework for Latin American economies requires external verification, as the paper strictly models a closed economy governed by domestic life-cycle savings and abstracts from the region's specific history of concentrated land ownership (*latifundia*) and its reliance on foreign capital flows.
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q02`: Applying these specific land reform and nationalization counterfactuals to Latin America requires external verification, as the region's historical agricultural structures (*latifundia*), export dependence, and reliance on foreign capital inflows violate the model's assumption of a closed economy strictly financed by domestic life-cycle savings.
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q03`: Applying these findings to Latin America requires external verification, as the paper structurally assumes a closed economy where domestic life-cycle savings alone constrain capital accumulation, abstracting from the region's reliance on foreign capital flows, external sovereign debt, and historical land concentration (*latifundia*).
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q04`: Evaluating these specific fiscal and financial policy interventions for Latin America requires external verification, as the paper strictly models a closed economy governed by domestic life-cycle savings, abstracting from the region's reliance on foreign capital flows and its particular history of concentrated land ownership.
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q05`: Evaluating this critique of collateral optimism for Latin American economies requires external verification, as the paper strictly models a closed economy governed by domestic life-cycle savings, abstracting from the region's specific history of concentrated land ownership (*latifundia*), external sovereign debt constraints, and reliance on foreign capital inflows.
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q06`: While the paper explicitly references Latin American structuralism via Prebisch and ECLA, its formal mathematical model relies on closed-economy domestic savings; applying its exact dynamics to Latin American localization-rent debates requires external verification regarding the region's exposure to foreign capital inflows and dollarized real estate markets.
- [PROJECT TRANSLATION] `PDF005_Q07`: The authors explicitly address "developing countries" and the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis regarding declining terms of trade, but applying these exact model limitations to Latin America requires external verification regarding the specific integration of Latin American peripheral economies into contemporary global financial and trade systems.
